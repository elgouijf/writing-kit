## B. Forms that read as machine-written (`banned.md`)

- B1 Em-dashes (—, `---`)
- B2 "rather than"
- B3 "X, not Y" antitheses ("skills to acquire, not products to deliver")
- B4 Reflexive hedging with "may" ("an account may no longer correspond") and uniform hedging
- B5 Overclaiming in a conclusion
- B6 "Therefore" closing every point
- B7 A paragraph or section ending on a maxim or a summary of itself ("The pair we can verify best is also the largest")
- B8 Aphoristic bold lead-ins ("An address never proves a company")
- B9 A slogan: a general moral with no agent ("Process is real, and it is there for a reason")
- B10 Tricolons everywhere ("faster, cheaper and more reliable")
- B11 Ordinals in a synthesis ("The first… The second… The third…")
- B12 Openers: "It is worth noting", "Notably", "Importantly", "In today's world", "plays a crucial role"
- B13 *leverage, delve, robust, seamless, comprehensive, showcase, underscore, highlight, serves as a, holistic*
- B14 Clefts ("it is X that…", "what matters is…"), "there is/are", "by -ing" openers, "not only"
- B15 Vague intensifiers (*key, critical, significant, essential, vital, powerful*)
- B16 Nominalisations (*the fact that, the use of, the ability to, in terms of, with respect to*)
- B17 `, -ing` adjuncts ("…, allowing the model to…")
- B18 Bare "This is / This shows" openers
- B19 A formula the author flagged, merely reworded
- B20 Dates and commit counts as narrative in a report whose period is on the cover
- B21 Questions or narration as titles
- B22 In academic English genres: contractions, "I think", "lots of", rhetorical questions, "etc."
